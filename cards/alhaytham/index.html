Ibn al-Haytham

Ibn al-Haytham showed that light travels in straight lines from objects into the eye, testing his ideas with careful experiments. His camera obscura is the pinhole camera model that computer vision still uses to link the pixels in an image to the real world.

Where the line goes

  1. c. 1020

    A dark room with a tiny hole

    In his Book of Optics, written in Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham argued that light travels from objects into the eye in straight lines. He tested it with lamps or candles outside a darkened room: light from each flame passed through a small opening and made its own spot on the far wall, upside down.

  2. the idea

    Rays cross at the hole

    Light from the top of the flame travels down through the hole and lands low on the wall; light from the base lands high. So the picture is inverted. A wider hole lets in more light but each point becomes a bigger spot, so the image is brighter yet blurrier. A deeper room makes it larger.

  3. 2012

    Pixels for a neural network

    A digital camera is a camera obscura with a grid of light sensors on the back wall. Computer vision still uses this pinhole model. In 2012 a convolutional network called AlexNet won the ImageNet contest by sliding small learned filters over pixels, finding edges first, then shapes, then whole objects.

Remix the line


      
Widen the pinhole and the image grows brighter but blurrier; deepen the room and the upside-down candle grows larger.

The person
Abu Ali al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham, known in Europe as Alhazen, was born in Basra and later worked in Cairo under the Fatimid caliphate. He wrote on mathematics, astronomy and physics, but is best known for his seven-volume Book of Optics, written in the early eleventh century. Translated into Latin, it influenced European scientists for centuries, including Roger Bacon and Johannes Kepler. Many details of his life, such as the story that he feigned madness to escape a caliph, come from later accounts and are uncertain.

    
    
The big idea
Many ancient thinkers believed eyes send out rays to see. Ibn al-Haytham argued the reverse: light travels in straight lines from objects into the eye. He tested his ideas with careful, repeatable experiments rather than relying on authority, an approach central to modern science. He described the camera obscura, a dark room with a tiny hole, where light from each point of a scene passes through the hole and forms an upside-down image on the far wall.

    
The line to AI
Computer vision still begins with his geometry. The standard 'pinhole camera model' describes how every point in a 3D scene projects along a straight line through a single point onto a flat image, exactly the camera obscura. Algorithms for 3D reconstruction, self-driving car perception and augmented reality use this projection, written as a camera matrix, to relate pixels to the real world. Convolutional neural networks and vision transformers then learn to recognise what those pixels show.

    
On the card
Light rays from his turban converge through a pinhole and flip into an inverted image on a grid of pixels, which feed into the stacked filters of a convolutional neural network.

    
Think about
Ibn al-Haytham warned that the seeker of truth should test even the books he admires. How should we test the answers an AI gives us?
