Thomas Bayes

Thomas Bayes worked out how to update a belief when new evidence arrives, combining a prior with the likelihood of the evidence. Published after his death in 1763, his theorem underpins probabilistic machine learning, from spam filters to AI that reports its own uncertainty.

Where the line goes

  1. 1763

    An essay found after his death

    Bayes died in 1761. His friend Richard Price found his essay on chance and read it to the Royal Society in 1763. It imagines a ball rolled onto a table out of sight, then more balls rolled, and asks what the results reveal about where the first one lies.

  2. the idea

    Belief that narrows

    The hidden ball is never seen. Each new ball reports only whether it stopped to the left or right of it. Belief about the hidden ball's position starts flat, then becomes a hump that grows narrower with every roll and settles over the true spot. Mathematicians call this curve a Beta distribution.

  3. today

    Learning from evidence

    Updating a belief as evidence arrives is the heart of probabilistic machine learning. Early spam filters used naive Bayes: each word nudges the chance an email is spam up or down. Bayesian methods also let AI systems say how unsure they are.

Remix the line


      
Move the hidden ball or roll more balls and watch the belief curve narrow around it; with only a few rolls the curve stays wide, and that width is honest uncertainty.

Bayes' theorem: updating beliefs as new evidence arrives.

    
    
The person
Thomas Bayes was an English Presbyterian minister who served a chapel in Tunbridge Wells. He studied logic and theology at the University of Edinburgh, as Nonconformists were barred from Oxford and Cambridge. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1742. His famous work was never published in his lifetime. After he died in 1761, his friend Richard Price edited it and presented it to the Royal Society in 1763. The only portrait said to show him may not be him.

    
    
The big idea
Bayes' theorem tells you how to update a belief when you get new evidence. Start with a prior: how likely something seemed before. Then ask how likely the evidence would be if it were true. Combine them and you get a posterior: your updated belief. For example, a positive test for a rare disease may still mean you are probably healthy, because the disease was so unlikely to begin with. Pierre-Simon Laplace later developed the idea much further.

    
The line to AI
Bayes' theorem is the basis of probabilistic machine learning. Early spam filters used naive Bayes classifiers: each word shifts the probability that an email is spam, and the filter updates its belief word by word. Bayesian methods let AI systems express uncertainty rather than giving one confident answer, which matters in medicine and self-driving cars. Bayesian optimisation is used to tune the settings of large models, and Bayesian neural networks treat each weight as a probability distribution rather than a single number.

    
On the card
The fold of his clerical collar becomes a wide prior bell curve that a beam of evidence narrows into a sharp posterior, which splits into the word-by-word probabilities of a naive Bayes spam filter.

    
Think about
Should an AI always tell you how uncertain it is? Would you trust a doctor, or a chatbot, more or less if it said it was only 70% sure?
