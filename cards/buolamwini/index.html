Joy Buolamwini

Joy Buolamwini's 2018 Gender Shades study with Timnit Gebru showed that commercial face-analysis systems were nearly perfect on lighter-skinned men but wrong on up to a third of darker-skinned women. It made bias auditing, testing AI separately on every group, standard practice.

Where the line goes

  1. 2018

    Gender Shades

    Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested face-analysis systems from IBM, Microsoft and Face++ on 1,270 photos of parliamentarians from three African and three European countries. The systems guessed gender almost perfectly for lighter-skinned men but got up to about a third of darker-skinned women wrong.

  2. the idea

    Split the score

    A single overall accuracy figure can look acceptable while hiding large failures. Gender Shades split the results into four groups by skin type and gender. Errors were under 1 per cent for lighter-skinned men but up to about a third for darker-skinned women, a gap that the average had concealed.

  3. today

    Bias audits

    After the study, all three companies released improved systems; a 2019 re-audit by Inioluwa Deborah Raji and Buolamwini measured the change. Testing a model separately on different groups is now standard in responsible AI, from model cards to proposed laws.

The person
Joy Buolamwini is a Canadian-American computer scientist who studied at Georgia Tech and was a Rhodes Scholar at Oxford. As a graduate student at the MIT Media Lab, she found that face-tracking software would not detect her face until she put on a white mask. That experience led to her research on bias in AI. In 2016 she founded the Algorithmic Justice League, which uses research, art and advocacy to expose harms from AI. Her work features in the documentary Coded Bias and her book Unmasking AI.

    
    
The big idea
In the 2018 Gender Shades study, Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial AI systems from IBM, Microsoft and Face++ that guessed whether a face was male or female. They built a balanced set of photos of parliamentarians from three African and three European countries. The systems were almost perfect on lighter-skinned men, with errors under 1 per cent, but got up to about a third of darker-skinned women wrong. Looking at skin type and gender together revealed failures that overall accuracy figures hid.

    
The line to AI
Gender Shades helped create the practice of bias auditing: testing an AI system separately on different groups of people instead of reporting a single accuracy number. After the study, the companies tested released improved systems, and several large firms later paused or limited sales of face recognition to police. Today, audits that break down results by group are a standard part of responsible AI, including in proposed laws and company model cards. The lesson applies to any model trained on unbalanced data.

    
On the card
Her line becomes a grid of faces sorted by skin type and gender, beneath which a bar chart of error rates climbs steeply for one group, and the chart becomes a fairness audit table comparing a model's accuracy across groups.

    
Think about
An AI can be 95 per cent accurate overall and still fail badly for some people. How should we decide whether a system is fair enough to use?
