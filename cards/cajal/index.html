Santiago Ramón y Cajal

Santiago Ramón y Cajal showed that the nervous system is built from separate cells, neurons, that pass signals in one direction across tiny gaps. Artificial neural networks borrow this picture in simplified form: separate units, weighted connections and one-way signal flow.

Where the line goes

  1. 1888

    Separate cells, tiny gaps

    Using Golgi's silver stain, which turns a few nerve cells black, Cajal drew thousands of them by hand. He showed the nervous system is made of separate cells, now called neurons, that almost touch at small gaps later named synapses. He shared the 1906 Nobel Prize with Golgi, who disagreed.

  2. the idea

    In through dendrites, out along the axon

    Cajal argued that signals flow one way: into the branching dendrites, through the cell body and out along the axon. A neuron receives pulses from many others at its synapses. Their charge adds up in the cell body and slowly leaks away; if it crosses a threshold, the neuron fires a spike down its axon to the next cells.

  3. 1943

    The artificial neuron

    Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts boiled this down to maths: multiply each input by a weight, add them up, and fire if the total passes a threshold. Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) learned the weights. Modern networks stack billions of such units, though real neurons are far more complex.

Remix the line


      
Turn up one synapse's strength or lower the threshold and the cell fires more often; set all three low and it falls silent, however many signals arrive.

The neuron doctrine and detailed drawings of brain cells.

    
    
The person
Santiago Ramón y Cajal was a rebellious boy who wanted to be an artist, but his father, a doctor, pushed him into medicine. He served as an army doctor in Cuba before becoming a professor in Spain. Using a silver-staining method invented by Camillo Golgi, which he improved, he studied nerve tissue under the microscope and made thousands of detailed drawings. He shared the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Golgi, even though the two disagreed about how the nervous system is built.

    
    
The big idea
Golgi thought the nervous system was one continuous web. Cajal showed it is made of separate cells, now called neurons, that nearly touch at tiny gaps (later named synapses). He also argued that signals flow in one direction: in through the branching dendrites, through the cell body, and out along the axon. This 'neuron doctrine' became the foundation of modern neuroscience.

    
The line to AI
Artificial neural networks borrow Cajal's picture, loosely. In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts modelled a neuron as a unit that adds up weighted inputs and fires if the total passes a threshold. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) learned those weights. Today's deep networks still use separate units, connection weights like synapse strengths, and one-way signal flow. The link is a simplification: real neurons are far more complex than an artificial neuron's weighted sum and activation function.

Think about
Cajal was also an artist. Did drawing by hand help him see things a photograph might not? What might we lose if AI does all our observing for us?
