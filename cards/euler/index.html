Leonhard Euler

Leonhard Euler proved in 1735 that no walk crosses each of Königsberg's seven bridges exactly once, by reducing the map to dots and lines. That idea founded graph theory, and graph neural networks now learn directly on such networks of nodes and edges.

Where the line goes

  1. 1735

    Seven bridges, one walk

    People in Königsberg asked whether anyone could stroll across all seven bridges over the Pregel river exactly once. Euler showed in 1735 that it cannot be done. He ignored distances and street shapes and kept only which pieces of land each bridge joined.

  2. the idea

    Count the lines at each dot

    Each piece of land becomes a dot and each bridge a line. Every time a walker passes through a dot, two lines are used, so only the start and finish can have an odd number. Königsberg had four odd dots, so the walker always gets stuck.

  3. today

    Graphs that learn

    Dots joined by lines are now called graphs. Knowledge graphs store facts as linked nodes, and graph neural networks learn on them: each node holds a vector and, layer by layer, passes messages to its neighbours. They help predict molecule properties and traffic, and power Google DeepMind's GraphCast weather model.

Graph theory, the number e, and much of modern mathematical notation.

    
    
The person
Euler was born in Basel and studied under Johann Bernoulli. He spent most of his career at the academies of St Petersburg and Berlin and is among the most productive mathematicians in history. He introduced notation still used today, including f(x) for a function, e, i and the use of π. He lost the sight of his right eye in 1738 and became almost totally blind from 1766, yet he kept working, dictating results and doing long calculations in his head. He died in St Petersburg in 1783.

    
    
The big idea
The city of Königsberg had seven bridges joining four areas of land. Could you walk across every bridge exactly once? Euler saw that the shape of the map did not matter, only which areas were connected. He drew each area as a dot and each bridge as a line. A walk entering and leaving a dot uses two lines, so every dot except the start and finish needs an even number of lines. All four had an odd number, so the walk is impossible.

    
The line to AI
Euler's dots and lines became graph theory: nodes joined by edges. Graph neural networks learn directly on this structure. Each node holds a vector, and in every layer it sends messages along its edges and updates itself from its neighbours. This message passing is used to predict properties of molecules, where atoms are nodes and bonds are edges, and in Google DeepMind's GraphCast weather model. Knowledge graphs, which store facts as linked entities, also rest on Euler's idea.

    
On the card
The curls of his wig become the four landmasses and seven bridges of Königsberg, which shrink into four dots with odd-numbered edges, then light up as a graph neural network passing glowing messages from node to node.

    
Think about
Euler threw away every detail of the map except the connections. What gets lost when we turn the real world into a graph for an AI to learn from?
