Carl Friedrich Gauss

Carl Friedrich Gauss developed least squares, choosing the curve with the smallest total of squared errors, and used it in 1801 to predict where the lost dwarf planet Ceres would reappear. Mean squared error, which trains many neural networks, is the same idea.

Where the line goes

  1. 1801

    Finding a lost planet

    Ceres was spotted in January 1801, then lost in the Sun's glare. From a few weeks of noisy sightings, the 24-year-old Gauss predicted where it would reappear, and it was found there on the last night of the year. His method, least squares, appeared in print in 1809.

  2. the idea

    Make the squares smallest

    Each error is the gap between an observation and the fitted curve. The best fit is the one whose squared gaps add up to the smallest total. Squaring stops positive and negative errors cancelling and punishes big misses most. Gauss linked the method to the bell-shaped normal distribution of errors.

  3. today

    The loss that trains AI

    Mean squared error, the average of those squared gaps, is the loss used to train linear regression and many neural networks. Training does not solve it in one go as Gauss did. It steps downhill, again and again, and the loss falls.

Least squares and the normal (Gaussian) distribution.

    
    
The person
Gauss was born into a poor family in Brunswick. His talent was so clear that the Duke of Brunswick paid for his education. At 24 he published Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, a landmark in number theory. He went on to transform astronomy, surveying and the study of magnetism, and directed the Göttingen Observatory from 1807 until his death. He published only work he considered perfect, so many of his ideas were found in his notebooks after he died.

    
    
The big idea
In 1801 astronomers lost sight of the dwarf planet Ceres behind the Sun's glare. From a handful of noisy observations, Gauss predicted where it would reappear, and Franz Xaver von Zach found it there. His tool was least squares: choose the curve that makes the sum of the squared errors as small as possible. Legendre published the method first, in 1805, which caused a priority dispute. Gauss went further and linked it to the bell-shaped normal distribution of errors.

    
The line to AI
Linear regression, often the first model taught in machine learning, is Gauss's least squares. Mean squared error, the loss function used to train many neural networks, is his sum of squared errors, and minimising it is exactly right when the noise follows his bell curve. Diffusion models, which generate images, go one step further: they add Gaussian noise to a picture step by step until it is pure static, then train a network to remove the noise, step by step, creating new images.

    
On the card
The line of his cap becomes scattered observations of Ceres with a best-fit curve and little squares drawn on each error, which collapse into a bell curve whose noise floods a picture and then drains away as a diffusion model denoises it.

    
Think about
Squaring errors punishes big mistakes much more than small ones. When might that be a good choice for an AI, and when might it be unfair?
