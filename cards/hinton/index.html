Geoffrey Hinton

Geoffrey Hinton helped show in 1986 how backpropagation lets neural networks learn by sending errors backwards to adjust every weight. With his students he built AlexNet in 2012, sparking the deep learning boom, and he shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Where the line goes

  1. 1986

    Send the error backwards

    In a famous Nature paper, Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams showed how a network with hidden units can learn. Measure the error at the output, pass it backwards through the network to find how much each weight was to blame, then nudge every weight a little to reduce it. Repeat.

  2. the idea

    Sharing out the blame

    A network makes a guess and the error is measured at the output. Backpropagation uses the chain rule from calculus to pass that error backwards, layer by layer, working out how much each weight contributed. Every weight is nudged a little to reduce the error. With enough hidden units, repeated nudges let a network learn tangled boundaries between categories.

  3. 2012

    The same rule, scaled up

    Hinton's students Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever trained AlexNet, a deep network with 60 million weights, on GPUs and won the 2012 ImageNet challenge by a wide margin. Every large model since, including language models with billions of weights, is trained by backpropagation and gradient descent.

Backpropagation, deep learning and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics.

    
    
The person
Geoffrey Hinton is a British-Canadian computer scientist who studied experimental psychology at Cambridge before a PhD in artificial intelligence at Edinburgh. For decades neural networks were unfashionable, and he kept working on them anyway, largely at the University of Toronto. He shared the 2018 Turing Award with Yoshua Bengio and Yann LeCun, and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield. In 2023 he left Google so he could speak openly about the risks of AI.

    
    
The big idea
A neural network is made of many simple units connected by numbers called weights. When it makes a mistake, how do you know which of the millions of weights to change? In 1986 Hinton, David Rumelhart and Ronald Williams showed in a famous Nature paper how backpropagation answers this. You measure the error at the output, then pass it backwards through the network, working out how much each weight contributed. Each weight is then nudged slightly to reduce the error. Repeat millions of times and the network learns.

    
The line to AI
Backpropagation combined with gradient descent is how almost every modern neural network is trained, from image recognisers to large language models. In 2012 Hinton's students Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever built AlexNet with him, training a deep convolutional network on GPUs; it won the ImageNet challenge by a wide margin and sparked the deep learning boom. Since 2023 Hinton has warned publicly about risks such as misuse, job loss and AI systems that might become hard for humans to control.

    
On the card
The lines of his face become a layered network of nodes, and an error signal flows backwards along every edge as glowing gradients while a ball rolls down a loss surface (gradient descent), which settles into the trained weights of a deep network.

    
Think about
Hinton helped invent the methods behind modern AI, then warned about its dangers. When should scientists speak up about the risks of their own work?
