Jack Kilby

Jack Kilby built the first working integrated circuit in 1958, making every component of a circuit from one piece of semiconductor instead of wiring parts together by hand. Today a single AI chip holds about 80 billion transistors made the same way.

Where the line goes

  1. 1958

    A whole circuit on one slab

    On 12 September 1958 at Texas Instruments, Kilby showed his managers a sliver of germanium holding a transistor, resistors and a capacitor, joined by tiny gold wires. Connected as a phase-shift oscillator, it put a steady sine wave on an oscilloscope. Every part was made from the same piece of semiconductor.

  2. the idea

    Beating the tyranny of numbers

    By the 1950s circuits needed thousands of separate parts soldered together by hand, and every joint could fail. Engineers called this the tyranny of numbers. Making all the parts together on one slab removed the joints. Months later Robert Noyce at Fairchild independently made a silicon version that was easier to mass-produce.

  3. 2022

    Eighty billion on one chip

    AlexNet, the 2012 network that sparked deep learning, was trained on two gaming GPUs with about 3 billion transistors each. Nvidia's H100 AI chip, announced in 2022, holds about 80 billion, all made together on one piece of silicon. Training a large language model runs thousands of these chips for weeks.

The person
Jack Kilby grew up in Kansas, where an ice storm that cut the power lines sparked his interest in radio and electronics. After serving as an army electronics technician in the Second World War, he studied electrical engineering and joined Texas Instruments in 1958. As a new employee without holiday leave, he worked through the summer on the problem of wiring together ever more components. He later co-invented the handheld calculator. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics with Zhores Alferov and Herbert Kroemer.

    
    
The big idea
By the 1950s, electronic circuits needed thousands of separate transistors, resistors and capacitors soldered together by hand, and every joint could fail. Engineers called this the 'tyranny of numbers'. Kilby's answer was to make all the components from one piece of semiconductor. On 12 September 1958 he showed a sliver of germanium producing a steady sine wave on an oscilloscope. A few months later, Robert Noyce at Fairchild independently invented a silicon version that was easier to mass-produce. Both are credited as inventors.

    
The line to AI
Modern AI runs on integrated circuits. A single high-end AI chip, such as Nvidia's H100 GPU, holds about 80 billion transistors on one piece of silicon, all made together rather than wired by hand. Training a large language model means running thousands of these chips for weeks, performing trillions of multiply-and-add operations per second each. Kilby's germanium chip had only a handful of components, but the principle of building a whole circuit on one slab is what makes that scale possible.

    
On the card
The lines of his face become the few hand-drawn wires on Kilby's germanium chip, which multiply and shrink into the dense grid of a GPU die tiled with thousands of matrix-multiply cores.

    
Think about
Kilby and Noyce invented the integrated circuit independently within months of each other. Why do you think inventions often appear in several places at once?
