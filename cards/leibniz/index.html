Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed calculus independently of Newton, gave it the notation dy/dx, and published binary arithmetic in 1703. Neural networks learn by applying the chain rule, written in his notation, layer by layer, on chips that store every number in binary.

Where the line goes

  1. 1703

    Only 0 and 1

    Leibniz published his explanation of binary arithmetic in 1703. Every number can be written with just two digits, and each place is worth twice the one to its right. He was delighted to find that the six-line hexagrams of the Chinese I Ching, sent to him by a missionary, match binary counting.

  2. the idea

    1 + 1 = 10

    Binary counting goes 0, 1, 10, 11, 100. Addition works column by column from right to left, as in ordinary arithmetic. In binary, 1 + 1 makes 10, so the column shows 0 and a 1 carries to the next column. That same tiny rule, repeated, adds any two numbers, and it is how a chip's circuits add.

  3. today

    The chain rule, again and again

    Leibniz's notation makes the chain rule look like fractions cancelling: dy/dx = dy/du · du/dx. Backpropagation, the way neural networks learn, multiplies these rates backwards through every layer to find how each weight affects the error. All of it runs on chips that store every number in binary.

1646–1716Born Leipzig, GermanyMathematics & Philosophy

    

    
Calculus notation, binary arithmetic, and dreaming of a reasoning machine.

    
    
The person
Leibniz was a philosopher, diplomat, lawyer and librarian as well as a mathematician. He developed calculus independently of Isaac Newton and published it in 1684. A bitter priority dispute followed, and a Royal Society report sided with Newton, darkening Leibniz's last years. Historians now credit both men. He also built the Stepped Reckoner, a mechanical calculator that could multiply. He spent much of his career serving the dukes of Hanover, where he died in 1716.

    
    
The big idea
Leibniz invented the notation for calculus we still use: dy/dx for a rate of change and the long S, ∫, for adding up infinitely many tiny pieces. His notation makes the chain rule look almost obvious: dz/dx = dz/dy × dy/dx. In 1703 he published an explanation of binary arithmetic, writing every number with only 0 and 1. He also dreamed of a calculus ratiocinator, a machine that could settle arguments by calculating.

    
The line to AI
Neural networks learn through backpropagation, which is the chain rule applied over and over. A network is a long chain of functions, and to know how much one weight affects the final error, you multiply the rates of change along the chain, layer by layer, exactly as dz/dx = dz/dy × dy/dx. Gradients are written in his notation. The computers that run this calculation store every number in his binary. His reasoning machine was a dream, not a design, but the idea is strikingly close.

    
On the card
The curls of his wig become a chain of linked boxes labelled dz/dy and dy/dx, whose arrows reverse and flow backwards through the layers of a neural network as gradients (backpropagation), each box flickering between 0 and 1.

    
Think about
Leibniz hoped a machine could settle any argument by calculation. Which kinds of disagreements could an AI help settle, and which never could be?
