Ada Lovelace

In 1843 Ada Lovelace published Note G, a step-by-step method for Babbage's Analytical Engine to calculate the Bernoulli numbers, often called the first computer program. Her claim that machines cannot originate anything is still debated about creative AI today.

Where the line goes

  1. 1843

    Note G

    Lovelace translated an article on the Analytical Engine and added her own notes, three times longer. In Note G she set out, as a table of numbered operations on the engine's variables V1, V2, V3 and so on, how it could calculate the Bernoulli numbers, including a loop that repeats. It is often called the first published program.

  2. the idea

    Each answer feeds the next

    Each row of her table took two variables from the engine's store, combined them and wrote the result to another variable. Every Bernoulli number is built from the ones before, so a loop reused earlier results. Her B7 is today's B8, because she numbered only the Bernoulli numbers that are not zero. Both equal −1/30.

  3. today

    Lady Lovelace's Objection

    She wrote that the engine has no pretensions to originate anything: it does only what we know how to order it to do. In 1950 Turing named this Lady Lovelace's Objection and replied that machines can still surprise us. People now ask the same of AI that writes stories and makes pictures.

The person
Ada Lovelace was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron and Anne Isabella Milbanke, who encouraged her to study mathematics and science, unusual for a girl in the early 1800s. At 17 she met the inventor Charles Babbage and became fascinated by his designs for calculating machines. In 1843 she translated an Italian article by Luigi Menabrea about Babbage's Analytical Engine and added her own notes, which ended up much longer than the article itself. She suffered poor health for much of her life and died aged 36.

    
    
The big idea
Babbage's Analytical Engine was a mechanical computer, designed but never built, controlled by punched cards. In her Note G, Lovelace wrote out step by step how the engine could calculate a sequence called the Bernoulli numbers, including loops that repeat operations. This is often called the first published computer program. She also saw something bigger: if symbols could stand for things like musical notes, a machine could work with far more than just numbers.

    
The line to AI
Lovelace also argued that the engine could not originate anything new; it could only do what we know how to order it to do. In 1950 Alan Turing answered this directly in his paper on machine intelligence, naming it Lady Lovelace's Objection and pointing out that machines can still surprise us. The same debate is alive today when people ask whether image generators and language models are truly creative or are recombining patterns from their training data.

    
On the card
The ringlets of her hair become a looping punched-card program for the Bernoulli numbers, whose loop arrows spiral outward into a generative model sampling new images and melodies, posing the question of machine creativity.

    
Think about
Lovelace said a machine can only do what we order it to do. When an AI writes a poem nobody asked for word by word, has it created something, or just followed orders?
