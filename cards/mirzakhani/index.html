Maryam Mirzakhani

Maryam Mirzakhani became the first woman to win the Fields Medal, in 2014, for her work on hyperbolic surfaces and the paths on them. Her work was pure mathematics, but hyperbolic geometry now helps some AI models store tree-like hierarchies of ideas.

Where the line goes

  1. 2014

    Paths on surfaces

    Mirzakhani won the Fields Medal for her work on hyperbolic surfaces and their moduli spaces, the space of all shapes a surface can take. With Alex Eskin she proved a deep result about how straight paths move through these spaces, now nicknamed the magic wand theorem, which answers questions about billiards in polygons.

  2. the idea

    Unfold the table

    A billiard ball bouncing in a polygon can be studied by reflecting the table across each edge it hits instead of bouncing the ball. The zigzag becomes one straight path through mirrored copies, and gluing the copies together forms a surface. Questions about billiards become questions about straight paths on surfaces, the dynamics Mirzakhani studied.

  3. 2017

    Room for a hierarchy

    The link to AI is indirect. Nickel and Kiela's Poincaré embeddings place words inside a hyperbolic disc, with general ideas near the centre and specific ones near the rim. There is exponentially more room towards the edge, so tree-like hierarchies such as animal, mammal, dog fit far better than in flat space.

1977–2017Born Tehran, IranMathematics

    

    
First woman to win the Fields Medal, for work on curved surfaces.

    
    
The person
Mirzakhani grew up in Tehran and won gold medals at the International Mathematical Olympiad in 1994 and 1995, the second with a perfect score. She studied at Sharif University and earned her PhD at Harvard in 2004 under Curtis McMullen. She became a professor at Stanford. In 2014 she was awarded the Fields Medal, the first woman and first Iranian to receive it, alongside Artur Avila, Manjul Bhargava and Martin Hairer. She died of cancer in 2017, aged 40.

    
    
The big idea
Mirzakhani studied hyperbolic surfaces, shapes like a doughnut with several holes that curve like a saddle at every point. On such a surface, the shortest loops are called closed geodesics. Her PhD found a formula for how the number of simple loops, ones that never cross themselves, grows with length. She also studied moduli spaces, the space of all possible shapes such a surface can take, and how paths move through them. She often worked by sketching large drawings on paper.

    
The line to AI
The link to AI is indirect. Mirzakhani's work is pure mathematics and was not about machine learning. But hyperbolic geometry, the curved geometry she studied, is now used in some AI models. In 2017, Maximilian Nickel and Douwe Kiela introduced Poincaré embeddings, which place words or concepts inside a hyperbolic disc. Tree-like hierarchies, such as animal, mammal, dog, fit much better in hyperbolic space than in flat space, because there is exponentially more room towards the edge.

    
On the card
The line of her hair becomes a saddle-shaped surface with several holes wrapped by non-crossing loops, which flattens into a Poincaré disc tiled with shrinking triangles where a tree of concepts branches towards the rim (hyperbolic embeddings).

    
Think about
Mirzakhani's work was pure mathematics with no application in mind, yet related ideas now appear in AI. Should we fund research that has no obvious use?
