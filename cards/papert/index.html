Seymour Papert

Seymour Papert co-created Logo, in which children program a turtle to make shapes, and argued that people learn best by building and debugging, an idea he called constructionism. That idea shapes debates about whether AI tutors should give answers or help students build.

Where the line goes

  1. 1967

    Logo and the turtle

    With Wally Feurzeig and Cynthia Solomon, Papert created Logo, a language children use to command a turtle that leaves a trail as it moves. Making a shape means thinking like the turtle: walk forward, turn, repeat. His 1980 book Mindstorms argued that children should program computers, not the other way round.

  2. the idea

    Turns add up to circles

    Every closed turtle path turns through a whole number of full turns. A square is 4 × 90°, one full turn, while a five-pointed star made with 144° turns adds up to two full turns, 720°. Children discover this by building and debugging rather than memorising, which is real geometry learned through making.

  3. today

    Learning by building

    Papert called this constructionism: people learn best by making things and debugging their mistakes. Now children can ask AI to write code for them. A tutor in his spirit asks questions and lets students predict, test and fix their own work, instead of simply handing over the answer.

Logo, turtle geometry and constructionism; co-author of Perceptrons.

    
    
The person
Seymour Papert grew up in South Africa, where he was active in the anti-apartheid movement as a student. After earning doctorates in mathematics, he worked in Geneva with the psychologist Jean Piaget, studying how children build understanding. At MIT he co-directed the Artificial Intelligence Laboratory with Marvin Minsky. With Wally Feurzeig and Cynthia Solomon he co-created the Logo programming language in 1967. His 1980 book Mindstorms argued that children should program computers, not the other way round. LEGO later named its robotics kits after it.

    
    
The big idea
In Logo, children give commands to a turtle, a small robot or screen cursor that draws as it moves: FORWARD 100, RIGHT 90. To draw a square, you must think like the turtle. Repeating four times shows why the turns add up to 360 degrees. Papert called this constructionism: people learn best by building things they care about and debugging their mistakes. With Minsky he also wrote Perceptrons in 1969, proving that single-layer neural networks cannot solve some simple problems, such as XOR.

    
The line to AI
Perceptrons is often said to have slowed neural network research in the 1970s, though how much blame it deserves is debated. Its limits applied to single-layer networks; multi-layer networks trained with backpropagation, popularised in 1986, overcame them, and that is what deep learning is built on. Papert's constructionism shapes debates about AI tutors today: does a chatbot that gives answers help students learn, or is it better used as something students build with, test and debug?

    
On the card
His line becomes a turtle tracing a square with four 90-degree turns, the path splitting into an XOR plot that no single straight line can separate, which a second hidden layer of a neural network finally divides.

    
Think about
If an AI tutor can explain anything instantly, what is still worth learning by building and debugging it yourself?
