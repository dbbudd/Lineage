Claude Shannon

Claude Shannon founded information theory in 1948, showing that information can be measured in bits and that surprise, or entropy, sets the limits of compression and communication. Language models are trained with cross-entropy loss, built directly on his measure of surprise.

Where the line goes

  1. 1948

    Fake English from statistics

    In A Mathematical Theory of Communication, Shannon built text by chance. Letters picked at random give noise. Letters picked by how often they occur, then by which letter tends to follow which, start to form words. His examples ran from XFOML RXKHRJFFJUJ to phrases that look almost like English.

  2. the idea

    Surprise, counted in bits

    A letter that is easy to guess carries little information; a surprising one carries a lot. Shannon measured the surprise of an outcome with probability p as −log₂ p bits, and called the average surprise of a source its entropy, H. The more context there is, the easier the next letter is to guess, so fewer bits are needed.

  3. today

    Next-token prediction

    A large language model plays Shannon's guessing game with tokens instead of letters, looking thousands of tokens back. It is trained with cross-entropy loss: the average surprise, in bits or nats, at each real next token. Learning means becoming less surprised by human writing.

Founding information theory and measuring information in bits.

    
    
The person
Claude Shannon grew up in a small Michigan town, tinkering with radios and model planes. In his 1937 master's thesis at MIT he showed that the true-or-false logic of Boolean algebra could be built from electrical switches, an insight at the heart of every digital circuit. At Bell Labs he published A Mathematical Theory of Communication in 1948, which founded the field of information theory. He was also famously playful, building juggling machines and a mechanical mouse called Theseus that could learn its way through a maze.

    
    
The big idea
Shannon showed that information can be measured, just like length or mass. The unit is the bit: the answer to one yes-or-no question. A message is only informative if it is surprising. If you can easily guess the next letter, it carries little information. He called the average surprise of a source its entropy. This let engineers calculate exactly how much a message can be compressed and how fast data can be sent reliably through a noisy channel, from phone lines to Wi-Fi.

    
The line to AI
Large language models are trained with cross-entropy loss, a measure built directly on Shannon's entropy: it counts, in bits, how surprised the model is by each real next token. Training means nudging the model to be less surprised. Shannon also anticipated language models. His 1948 paper generated fake English from letter and word statistics, and in his 1951 paper Prediction and Entropy of Printed English, people guessed text one letter at a time so he could estimate how predictable English is. Next-token prediction is exactly that game.

    
On the card
His hairline becomes a branching probability tree of letters, each branch weighted by p, whose surprises -log p are summed into an entropy curve that flows into a language model's falling cross-entropy loss as it learns to predict the next token.

    
Think about
Shannon's theory measures how surprising a message is, not what it means. Can a system that only predicts the next word ever truly understand meaning?
