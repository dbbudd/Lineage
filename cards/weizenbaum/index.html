Joseph Weizenbaum

Joseph Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA, the first chatbot, which convinced many users it understood them using only keywords and templates. That tendency, now called the ELIZA effect, is stronger than ever with modern chatbots, as he warned in Computer Power and Human Reason.

Where the line goes

  1. 1966

    ELIZA

    At MIT, Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA, a program people could type to, described in a 1966 paper. Its DOCTOR script copied a style of therapist who reflects a patient's words back. ELIZA understood nothing, yet Weizenbaum was shocked that people confided in it as if it understood them, and he became a leading critic.

  2. the idea

    Keyword, pattern, template

    ELIZA looks for the highest-ranked keyword it knows, cuts the sentence around it, swaps I for you and my for your, and pours the pieces into a stock template. A sentence about feeling sad about one's family might get a request to say more about family. There is no meaning anywhere in the process.

  3. today

    The ELIZA effect

    Reading understanding into a program is now called the ELIZA effect. Large language models are vastly more capable than keyword rules, which makes the effect stronger: people trust their answers and form attachments to them. Weizenbaum argued some human judgements should never be handed to machines.

ELIZA, the first chatbot, and warnings about trusting computers.

    
    
The person
Joseph Weizenbaum was born in Berlin to a Jewish family, which fled Nazi Germany for the United States in 1936. After serving in the US Army Air Corps during the Second World War, he studied mathematics and worked on early computers, including a banking computer system. He became a professor at MIT, where he wrote ELIZA. Shocked by how people reacted to it, he became one of the first and most outspoken critics of over-trusting computers, especially in his 1976 book Computer Power and Human Reason.

    
    
The big idea
ELIZA, written in the mid-1960s and described in a 1966 paper, was a program you could type to. Its best-known script, DOCTOR, imitated a style of therapist who mostly reflects your words back. ELIZA had no understanding at all. It spotted keywords and rearranged your sentence using simple templates, so typing that you feel sad about your family might get a reply asking you to tell it more about your family. Even so, many users became convinced that it understood them.

    
The line to AI
People's tendency to believe a program understands them when it does not is now called the ELIZA effect. Modern chatbots are vastly more capable than ELIZA because they are large language models trained on huge amounts of text, not simple keyword rules. But the effect is stronger than ever: people form emotional attachments to chatbots and may trust their answers too much. Weizenbaum argued that some decisions, such as judgements involving care and respect, should never be handed to computers.

    
On the card
His beard becomes a branching keyword-matching flowchart that reflects a sentence back through a simple template, and the flowchart expands into a modern chat window where a user leans in, trusting the reply (the ELIZA effect).

    
Think about
Is it harmful for people to feel that a chatbot understands or cares about them? Where would you draw the line?
