Gladys West

Gladys West programmed computers at the US Navy's Dahlgren base to build precise models of the Earth's lumpy shape from satellite data. Accurate GPS depends on such models, and drones, delivery robots and self-driving cars rely on that accuracy.

Where the line goes

  1. 1978

    Measuring a lumpy planet

    At the US Navy base in Dahlgren, Virginia, Gladys West programmed computers to process satellite data, including radar measurements from Seasat and later Geosat, into ever more precise models of the Earth's shape. Those models of the geoid helped make satellite positioning accurate.

  2. the idea

    Ranges, then a model

    Each satellite's signal gives a distance, so the receiver lies somewhere on a sphere around that satellite. Where the spheres meet is the position, and a fourth satellite fixes the receiver's clock error. A model of the Earth then turns that point into a height. Treating the Earth as a perfect sphere would be out by over a kilometre.

  3. today

    Knowing where the ground is

    Drones, delivery robots and self-driving cars combine satellite positioning with cameras, lidar and motion sensors. Satellite fixes are only useful to them if the Earth model behind them is right to within metres. That accuracy rests on decades of geodesy, including West's work.

The person
Gladys West grew up on a farm in rural Virginia in a family of African American sharecroppers. Finishing top of her high school class won her a scholarship to Virginia State College, where she studied mathematics. In 1956 she joined the US Navy's base at Dahlgren, Virginia, as one of only four Black employees, and worked there for 42 years. Her contribution became widely known only after she retired. She was inducted into the US Air Force Space and Missile Pioneers Hall of Fame in 2018, and died in 2026 aged 95.

    
    
The big idea
The Earth is not a perfect sphere. It bulges at the equator, and because gravity varies, even 'sea level' is lumpy. This bumpy surface is called the geoid. From the 1970s, West programmed computers to process satellite measurements and build increasingly precise models of the geoid, including work on data from the Seasat and Geosat radar satellites. Knowing the Earth's exact shape is essential for working out exactly where a satellite, or a person, is.

    
The line to AI
GPS receivers work out position from satellite signals, but the answer is only as good as the model of the Earth behind it. West was one of many people whose geodesy work fed into those models. Self-driving cars, delivery drones and field robots use satellite positioning as one input, fused with cameras, lidar and motion sensors using algorithms such as the Kalman filter, while neural networks interpret what the cameras see.

    
On the card
The line of her shoulders becomes the bumpy outline of the geoid, ringed by satellite orbits, which narrows to a self-driving car's position estimate shrinking inside a cloud of uncertainty as sensor data are fused.

    
Think about
West's work was used by millions of people for decades before most of them knew her name. Whose hidden work do you rely on every day without realising it?
